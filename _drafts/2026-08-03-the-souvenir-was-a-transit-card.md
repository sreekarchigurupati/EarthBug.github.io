## The card

<!-- TODO: the scene. Where you bought it, what the machine looked like, why this card and not another. -->

Every transit card is the same object underneath. A MIFARE chip, an antenna coiled around the inside edge of a rectangle, a stored value that goes down when you tap. The technology is a solved problem and has been for two decades. There is no engineering left to do here. Which is exactly why the card is interesting.

Because once the function is settled, everything that remains is a choice. Someone chose the illustration. Someone chose the color, and how the value would be printed, and what the back would say in four languages. None of those choices make the turnstile open faster. All of them are the reason the card is in my desk drawer and not in a bin at Changi.

## What automation actually ate

<!-- TODO: your own framing here. The version I'd argue: -->

The anxious version of this conversation says automation is coming for creativity. I think it's coming for something adjacent and more specific: it's coming for *execution*, the part of the work where you already know what you want and now you have to make it exist. Rendering. Drafting. The fourteenth revision of the same layout at a slightly different aspect ratio.

That part was never the creative part. It just took so long that it felt like the job.

What's left when execution gets cheap is the part that was always the job:

- **Deciding what deserves to exist at all.** A machine will happily produce the thing you asked for. It has no opinion about whether it should have been made.
- **Taste**, which is not a mystical quality — it's a long-run record of choices you were willing to defend.
- **Knowing the context.** The four languages on the back of that card are not a design flourish. They're a fact about Singapore that someone had to know.

## The turnstile doesn't care

Here's the part I keep turning over. The card's *function* is perfectly automated — tap, beep, gate. The system that reads it is indifferent to everything a designer did. It cannot see the illustration. It cannot read the languages.

So every bit of design on the surface of that card exists for a reader the automation doesn't acknowledge: a person.

That's the pattern I think holds generally. Automation takes over the layer that machines can evaluate, and it turns out that layer is smaller than we feared. The layer above it — the one addressed to humans, that a machine can imitate but not *check* — is where the work moved. Not because designers retreated there, but because it's the only part that was ever addressed to us in the first place.

## The souvenir test

A useful question for anything you make: if it stopped working tomorrow, would anyone keep it?

Most things fail this. Most things should — a hammer that doesn't hammer is scrap. But some objects pass, and the ones that do pass for reasons that have nothing to do with what they were built for. The card in my drawer has a zero balance and expires eventually. It is, functionally, a dead object. I kept it anyway.
